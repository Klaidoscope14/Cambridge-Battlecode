CRITIQUE: FLAWS IN THE BATTLECODE STRATEGY PLAN
=================================================

1. OVERALL STRATEGIC FLAWS
--------------------------
- The plan is overconfident about several game mechanics and strategic assumptions that have not yet been verified against the rules or tested in the engine.
- The plan attempts to implement too many systems at once. This creates a high risk of producing a complicated bot with brittle interactions before a reliable scoring baseline exists.
- The strategy lacks a rigorous experimental-validation framework. Many proposed ideas are plausible hypotheses, but the plan does not yet establish how their actual impact will be measured.
- Optimizing for gross gems collected or nominal farming speed can be misleading. The important outcome is the team's final score, which depends on whether collected gems are successfully tributed and retained.

2. BANKING, CARRY RISK, AND TRIBUTE THROUGHPUT
----------------------------------------------
- A fixed carry threshold does not capture the changing risk of carrying gems. The danger can vary with health, distance to an altar, visible enemies, enemy mobility and abilities, and uncertainty caused by fog of war.
- A policy that recalls or banks based only on carried-gem count can make poor decisions when the route is dangerous, an altar is contested, or a safer alternative altar is available.
- Altar count alone is a weak proxy for effective tribute throughput. The number of altars does not by itself account for travel distance, route bottlenecks, safety, enemy access, or the chance that tribute will be interrupted.
- The plan may overestimate the benefit of maps with many altars. Numerous altars do not necessarily make banking safe or effortless if their approaches are exposed or opponents can contest them.
- The exact tribute-channel duration, interruption conditions, and recall interactions are critical to the strategy. If the assumptions about these mechanics are wrong, banking and survival policies may fail.

3. MAP IDENTIFICATION AND MAP ARCHETYPES
----------------------------------------
- The proposed map fingerprint `(width, height, team_size, symmetry)` may not uniquely identify a map. Different layouts can share all of those properties.
- The proposed map archetypes are hypotheses rather than established strategic facts. For example, a bush-heavy map does not automatically favor assassins, an open map does not automatically favor ranged poke, and a small map does not automatically favor AoE champions.
- Grouping maps based on simple counts—such as altar count, bush count, wall count, or map size—can hide important geometry. The placement of camps, altars, walls, and chokepoints may matter more than the aggregate counts.
- A strategy tuned to the known set of nine maps may overfit that pool and perform poorly on unfamiliar layouts or changed map configurations.
- Symmetry-based inference can be misleading if the bot treats inferred positions as certain observations, especially when the inferred information concerns dynamic state rather than static terrain.

4. DRAFTING AND CHAMPION SELECTION
----------------------------------
- The champion names and role assignments in the plan are not backed by quantified evidence in the current strategy document.
- The default draft spine and counterdrafts may fail when the assumed champion strengths, matchups, or team synergies do not hold in actual play.
- Rules such as selecting Marcella in response to stun-heavy opponents, or selecting assassins to punish carriers, are matchup hypotheses rather than proven counterdraft rules.
- Champion value cannot be judged only by role labels. A champion's actual value may depend on camp-clear speed, survivability, mobility, PvP performance, ability cooldowns, and how well the champion complements the rest of the team.
- Map-level features alone may not be enough to determine a good draft. The opponent's draft and the interaction between both teams' compositions can change the value of a pick.

5. STATE MACHINE AND DECISION PRIORITIES
----------------------------------------
- The strict priority chain—SURVIVE/FLEE, RECALL, TRIBUTE, GANK, DEFEND/PEEL, CAPTURE, FARM_CAMP, SHOP, WARD/SCOUT, EXPLORE/REPOSITION—can create priority inversions. A lower-level action may be strategically more valuable than a higher-priority action in a particular situation.
- A rigid priority chain can cause oscillation. A champion may repeatedly switch targets or objectives when small changes in health, distance, or observations alter which condition has priority.
- The plan mixes several distinct concerns: emergency constraints, strategic objective selection, and tactical action choice. Treating them as one flat list can make behavior difficult to reason about and debug.
- Role-specific priorities can conflict with team-wide needs. For example, a champion following its individual farm or hunt priority may leave an important team objective undefended.
- The plan does not yet establish how objective commitment is maintained when observations fluctuate or the bot temporarily loses visibility of a target.
- Channeling, recall, cooldowns, action legality, and other action-state constraints can interact with the priority system. Incorrect handling can cause wasted turns or prevent intended actions.

6. COMBAT MICRO AND MECHANICAL ASSUMPTIONS
------------------------------------------
- The combat section contains several mechanical assumptions that need verification, including monster range and vision, monster sleep behavior, attack-versus-move tradeoffs, ability action consumption, attack resets, stun effects, and champion-specific immunities or cooldown resets.
- Kiting is not universally optimal. Moving may avoid damage but can also sacrifice attacks, delay camp clears, or increase the time spent exposed to enemies.
- The value of a stun depends on what actions it prevents and for how long, as well as the opportunity cost of using the ability. A stun is not automatically valuable merely because it disables an opponent.
- The plan's assumptions about ranged champions attacking while moving, or about ability use resetting or preserving attacks, can cause systematic damage or tempo losses if inaccurate.
- Champion-specific claims—such as Marcella's stun immunity or Vendetta's on-kill cooldown reset—must match the actual rules and engine behavior.
- Hidden enemies and bush interactions introduce uncertainty. Treating unseen enemies as absent can lead to unsafe routes, failed ganks, and lost carried gems.
- Retrying after a blocked or invalid move is not just a combat concern; if movement failures are not handled consistently, they can waste turns across farming, fleeing, and objective control.

7. TEAM COORDINATION AND SHARED MEMORY
-------------------------------------
- The draft-memory and shared-memory design is vulnerable to stale or overwritten information if several champions update shared intent without a clear ownership or freshness model.
- The shared team memory is only 8 bytes, so it cannot hold a detailed team state. Overly ambitious coordination data may be compressed so aggressively that it becomes ambiguous or unreliable.
- The plan does not fully resolve how conflicting assignments, duplicate target selection, or simultaneous changes in team priorities will be represented.
- Shared state may become stale when a target dies, a camp is cleared, an altar becomes contested, or a champion changes role or objective.
- Static draft memory and dynamic team state have different lifetimes. Mixing them can leave the bot using an outdated opening plan after the match situation has changed.
- Coordination can create overhead or failure modes of its own. More communication does not automatically produce better decisions if messages are stale, contradictory, or based on incomplete observations.

8. SYMMETRY AND FOG-OF-WAR INFERENCE
------------------------------------
- Symmetry can help infer likely static locations, but it does not reveal dynamic state with certainty. An inferred enemy position is not the same as a currently observed enemy.
- If the bot fails to distinguish observed facts from inferred possibilities, it may make overly confident decisions about enemy locations, camp state, or objective safety.
- Inference confidence and information freshness are not sufficiently specified in the plan. Old observations can become unreliable as champions move and objectives change.

9. ENGINEERING COMPLEXITY AND WASMTIME FUEL
-------------------------------------------
- The architecture includes perception management, state machines, symmetry handling, danger-aware pathfinding, memory encoders, opportunistic actions, map adaptation, combat tactics, and multiple strategic roles. Implementing all of these simultaneously increases integration risk.
- The plan risks spending engineering effort on sophisticated features before confirming that basic movement, combat, camp capture, banking, and survival work reliably.
- Fuel-aware optimizations are proposed without a clear profile of which computations actually consume significant fuel. Premature optimization can complicate code without improving performance.
- Opportunistic use of leftover turn fuel may introduce inconsistent behavior or unexpected action interactions if it is not carefully constrained.
- Porting patterns from a previous game or codebase can be dangerous if the underlying action model, memory semantics, turn structure, or mechanics differ. Old-game assumptions should not silently carry over.

10. TESTING AND EVIDENCE GAPS
-----------------------------
- The plan does not define a sufficiently explicit experimental framework for deciding whether a feature improves results.
- Without tracking outcomes such as win rate, average tributed gems, gems lost on death, gems per 100 rounds, camp-clear time, travel overhead, tribute completion rate, deaths, and fuel use, it will be difficult to identify why a bot wins or loses.
- Aggregate results can be misleading if tests do not cover all maps, both team sides, and varied opponent styles.
- A single successful match or a few hand-picked examples cannot establish that a strategy is generally strong.
- Several design choices—draft counters, map archetypes, carry thresholds, kiting, ganking, and altar denial—need to be treated as hypotheses until their results are measured.
- Without controlled comparisons between versions, improvements in one subsystem may hide regressions in another.

11. SCOPE AND IMPLEMENTATION RISK
---------------------------------
- The initial implementation plan is too broad for a first reliable version. Draft adaptation, map classification, multiple roles, risk scoring, ganking, altar denial, symmetry inference, shopping, combat micro, and coordination all create separate failure modes.
- A complicated bot can appear sophisticated while underperforming a simpler bot that consistently farms, survives, captures camps, and tributes gems.
- The plan does not sufficiently distinguish essential scoring functionality from optional features whose value remains unproven.
- Integration bugs between individually reasonable systems may be more damaging than the absence of any one advanced feature.

OVERALL VERDICT
---------------
The strategy has a strong economic focus, but its main weaknesses are unverified mechanical assumptions, overly coarse banking and map heuristics, a potentially brittle priority system, insufficient evidence for draft and combat rules, excessive initial scope, and a lack of systematic testing. These are the flaws in the current plan; this document intentionally excludes proposed fixes and recommendations.
